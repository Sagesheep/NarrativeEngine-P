# Heroes Aftermath — Design Pillars

**File purpose:** Scratchpad / source-of-truth for the campaign's spine before the engine-ready `world_lore_*.md` is generated. Captures the player backstory, the Series 1 roughline that shapes Series 2, and the six villain-seat insertion model.

**Status:** Draft — names, locations, and power systems pending. Locked: the core inversion (heroes → tyrants), the isekai mechanism, and the seat framework.

---

## 0. CAMPAIGN CODELINE

A two-series structure. The player only plays Series 2, but Series 1 is the history that made the world they land in.

- **Series 1 (history):** The Orc Lord War. 9 heroes + 1 protagonist (all ~16 years old) rose, freed humanity from orc enslavement. 4 of the 9 died. The 5 survivors became the Five Sages. The protagonist is conspicuously absent from the record — a mystery (§1.3).
- **Series 2 (the campaign):** 12 years after the war. The 5 survivors (now 28 years old) rule as the Five Sages — iron-handed, and **stronger than they were in Series 1** because they are adults now, not teenagers. The player is transmigrated into a villain's body. The Series 2 hero (22 years old, native, was 10 when the war ended) is the player's *opponent* — the rebel coming to kill the sages, including possibly the player. The word *villain* is reassigned: the branded may be the actual righteous.

**The thesis:** Power corrupts the righteous. The sage who topples a sage becomes the next sage. The cycle is the antagonist — no single villain.

## 1. PLAYER BACKSTORY (LOCKED)

This is the player's pre-campaign identity. Identical for every playthrough — only the villain-seat they land in changes.

1. **Series 1 is a single-player game** — a full-dive virtual reality RPG (think FF14-tier sensory immersion, but solo). It has been released for **3 years** and the community is still finding new things in it. The world is deep; the secrets are layered.
2. The player is a **salaryman/woman who streams casually at night after work.** Not a pro, not a speedrunner — a regular person who loves this game and shares it with a small chat. Intimate, not spectacle.
3. On this particular stream, the player triggers the **hidden true ending** — a secret finale that, after 3 years and hundreds of thousands of players, nobody has found. They are the first.
4. After the ending credits, the game displays an in-world prompt: *"Congratulations. You are the first player to find the true hidden ending. Do you wish to see the second series as a prize? Streaming will be forcibly disabled."*
5. Like any fan who just found the secret ending of a game they love, they say yes. **The prize is knowledge** — they want to live in the sequel, to carry what they know from Series 1 into Series 2. That knowledge is the reward. It is also the trap.
6. **The player's in-game choices carry no moral weight *to the player*.** They made all the decisions — including letting the 4 die for the true ending — as a *gamer*. Like killing pedestrians in GTA, there's no guilt because it's a game. The characters aren't real. The deaths are pixels. This is critical: the player is not a monster. They are a normal person playing a game normally. The horror arrives *later*, in Series 2, when the pixels become people and the "true ending" becomes a body count the player chose.
7. The player suffers a heart attack at home, alone. (In-world: the no-return / summoning mechanism. There is no going back.) The stream goes dark mid-broadcast. The chat witnesses the disappearance. There is no body found.
8. Everything goes black.
9. The player wakes up transmigrated into **VILLAIN X** (the villain seat they chose at campaign meta-start). The campaign begins.

### 1.1 THE ISEKAI MECHANISM (LOCKED)

- **The player is the only transmigrator.** No party, no other arrivals, no "find your friends" arc. The isolation is the tension. The player is alone in a world that shouldn't exist, in a body that isn't theirs, with knowledge no one alive should have.
- **In the game, the player controlled the Series 1 hero** — a custom-built protagonist (sandboxy, free build, Skyrim-style). But **in Series 2, the player is transmigrated into a villain's body, NOT into the Series 1 hero.** The hero the player played is someone else in canon — or maybe no one. The player is not the hero this time. They are the villain.
- **The player is NOT one of the 9 heroes.** The 9 are the canon companion roster the player recruited in the game. The framing is *betrayal* ("I admired these people, now I'm the one they're hunting"), not *guilt* ("I helped build this tyranny"). The player was a fan of the heroes. Now they are the enemies of the heroes.
- **The Series 2 hero is native.** No isekai mumbo jumbo for the hero. The hero of Series 2 is of this world — born in it, shaped by it, fighting for it. The player is the anomaly; the hero is the native. This keeps the world grounded and makes the player's foreignness the source of tension, not the hero's.
- **The player chooses the villain seat (meta); the MC experiences it as random.** At campaign start, the player picks which of the six seats (A–F) to occupy. In-fiction, the character has no idea why they woke up in *this* body and not another. The randomness is the in-fiction mystery — no "chosen one" explanation, no prophecy, no reason. The player knows they chose; the character doesn't. That gap is a load-bearing dramatic irony.
- **The prize is Series 1 knowledge.** The player clicked "yes" because they wanted to carry their Series 1 knowledge into the sequel. That knowledge is valuable and dangerous (see §1.2 — the game-vs-canon gap). The knowledge is the reward and the noose.

### 1.1.1 THE DISCOVERY RULE (LOCKED) — The Player Does Not Know They Are The Villain

**The player has ZERO Series 2 knowledge.** This is not a minor constraint — it defines the campaign opening. When the player wakes up transmigrated, they do NOT know:

- That they are "the villain."
- That there is a hero (P2, the 22-year-old rebel) coming to kill the sages.
- That there is a rebellion forming.
- What their body's reputation, role, or station is.
- Which seat (A–F) they occupy.
- Which specific villain body they are in.
- What the current political landscape looks like.

**The villain card is GM-only.** The player picks a seat *tier* (A–F) at meta-start — "I want to be a final-boss-tier villain," "I want to be a mid-boss-tier villain," etc. The SPECIFIC villain identity/body/role is determined by the GM and kept in GM notes — never shown to the player. The player wakes up in a body and discovers *which* villain they are through play. The Discovery Rule extends to the specific identity, not just the role.

**The player wakes up in a body and has to figure everything out.** The opening is a discovery phase, not a briefing. The player's experience is: *"I woke up in a world based on the game I played. The sages are ruling now (I can see that). Who am I? Where am I? What's going on? What's my role here?"*

**The "villain" framing comes from OUTSIDE.** The player discovers they are the villain through play — by:
- Seeing how the world reacts to their body (fear, hatred, deference, branded status).
- Meeting the hero (P2) and realizing P2 is coming for them.
- Discovering their body's history, reputation, or crimes.
- Being called a villain, tyrant, monster, or enemy by NPCs.
- Realizing their body is one of the sage's enforcers, or a sage themselves, or a sealed threat, or a branded rebel.

**The campaign opens with mystery, not with an evil lair.** The player is not handed a villain card. They wake up confused and discover their role through the world's reaction to them. The horror of "I am the villain" lands as a reveal, not a premise.

**Seat F is the extreme case (LOCKED):** Seat F — the fallen ally — is NOT a starting condition. It is an EMERGENT outcome. Seat F does not exist at campaign start. It is triggered by the hero (P2) making the sages' choice (the dark-route call, §3.3 open) during play. The player in Seat F starts as an ally (or potential ally) of the hero and BECOMES the villain through the hero's action. The player won't know they are "Seat F" because Seat F hasn't happened yet. The player thinks they're an ally. The hero's choice turns them. This is the seat that most directly embodies the thesis: the cycle repeats, and the player is the evidence.

### 1.1.2 THE MASK SAGE (LOCKED) — The Player's Landing Point

**One of the 5 sages is the "mask sage"** — a sage whose public identity is unknown. The public knows the masked sage exists and rules, but does not know which of the 9 war heroes is behind the mask. **This slot exists on purpose to give the player a landing point into Series 2.** The other 4 sages are publicly known (their war identities are on record); a transmigrator wearing any of their faces would be caught immediately as an impostor. Only the mask sage can host a transmigrator, because the mask conceals the identity gap — nobody knows who's supposed to be under it, so nobody can tell the mind behind it has changed.

**The masked hero is human, masked.** Not a non-human hiding racial features — a human who wears a mask to conceal identity. The mask is physical, Utawarerumono-style; in this world, masked figures are socially accepted, and people do not demand to see the face. A voice-magic modulator alters the wearer's voice. Even someone who knew the hero's voice during the war cannot connect the masked sage's voice to them.

**The masked hero was conveniently never in the same battle as the Series 1 protagonist.** Always behind the scenes — supporting, coordinating, operating in the war's shadow while the protagonist and the other 8 fought in the open. This is a deliberate worldbuilding construction, not an accident: it keeps the masked sage as a wild card. The player's game-avatar (the Series 1 protagonist) never fought directly alongside this hero, so the player's direct knowledge of them is minimal — what other companions said, what distant cutscenes showed, what lore books mentioned. The blind spot is designed into the war's history. The player can't rely on game-knowledge to understand this sage because the game itself gave them almost nothing.

**The beer-and-moonlight moments still happened.** There is no rule against sharing a drink with a masked buddy. The masked hero could have had cutscene moments with the protagonist or other companions — camaraderie doesn't require an unmasked face. The mask is a barrier to *identification*, not to *connection*. This means the player may have warm game-memories of a masked figure they never saw — and in Series 2, they are inside that mask.

**The transmigration workflow:** If the player wants to be a sage (Seat C, final boss tier, or any seat that places them in a sage's body), they are placed in the mask sage. The player defines the masked persona's presented appearance (the mask's design, the robes, the bearing) and behavior. The body under the mask is the original hero's, but since nobody sees it and nobody knows who it was, the player's identity is what the world interacts with. If the player does NOT want a sage seat, the mask sage is an NPC — the GM plays the unknown hero behind the mask, and the player is placed in a different villain body.

### 1.2 THE CANON-WORLD RULE (LOCKED) — The Events Are Real; The Protagonist Is Not

**Series 2's world is the canon world that the game was based on.** The game is a fictionalized version of real history. But the fictionalization is NARROW — it applies to exactly one thing.

**What IS canon (the player's knowledge is reliable):**
- The Orc Lord War happened. The 9 heroes existed. The 4 deaths happened — really, to real people, in the ways the game depicted (to teenage versions). The 5 survivors became the sages. The battles, the turning points, the scripted events the player remembers — all real. The sages' teenage kits, weaknesses, and habits — all real (though now 12 years out of date; see §2.3 power curve).
- The hidden ending's secret is real: the 5 chose to let the 4 die to win. That is canon history, not game fiction. The developers encoded a true atrocity into the secret ending.

**What is NOT canon (the player's knowledge is unreliable):**
- **The protagonist.** The player controlled a custom-built protagonist in the game — their character, their build, the figure they played for hundreds of hours. In canon, who was that? Was it one of the 9? A 10th figure? Nobody? Someone the sages reference but can't agree on? **This is the black box.** The player knows everything about the war except who THEY were in it. The most intimate game-memory is the hole.

**The inversion:** The player's knowledge of the *events* is reliable (to teenage versions). The player's knowledge of the *protagonist* — their own character, the one thing they should know best — is the one thing they don't know. The discrepancy between game and canon is not spread across the whole war; it is concentrated in a single figure. That makes the mystery sharper and the reveal heavier.

**The tell (revised):** When the player references the war, the battles, the deaths — they are mostly RIGHT, and natives will react with "how do you know that?" (suspicious but not impossible — a scholar could know). When the player references the protagonist — "my character," "the one who led them," "the bishop's lover" — they are on uncertain ground, and natives will react with "who told you about them?" (a different, more dangerous reaction — because the sages have spent 12 years making sure nobody talks about that figure). War-knowledge makes the player a suspicious scholar. Protagonist-knowledge makes the player a threat the sages will kill.

**The sandbox frame:** The game was open — sandboxy, free-build, scripted events embedded in a world you could ignore. The player spent hundreds of hours exploring. They know side quests, optional dungeons, NPC rumors, hidden bosses, lore books. Because the events are canon, most of this side content is ALSO canon — the optional dungeons were real ruins, the hidden bosses were real threats, the lore books were real texts. The player's deep lore knowledge is more reliable than they expected. The only thing that is NOT reliable is who they were.

### 1.3 THE SERIES 1 PROTAGONIST MYSTERY (LOCKED as mystery; answer is GM-generated)

**The Series 1 protagonist is the ONLY invalid element.** Everything else the player remembers — the war, the 9 heroes, the 4 deaths, the battles, the lore — is canon and reliable (to teenage versions). The protagonist is the one thing the player should know best and the one thing they don't know. In the game, this was their character — custom-built, hundreds of hours, the figure the player inhabited. In canon, who was that? **This is the black box.**

**The bishop-heroine hook (LOCKED):** In the game, the protagonist was in a relationship with Heroine 1 (the main love interest → now the Sage-Priest / bishop). In Series 2, the bishop references the protagonist obliquely — but the details are wrong, conflicting, or missing. *If the protagonist existed, and the bishop loved them, why is there no public record of them? Why do the 5 sages each remember them differently?* This is the player's entry point into the mystery. The bishop is the most emotionally loaded person to ask — and the most dangerous, because the bishop has spent 12 years burying this.

**The player will NOT be told the answer. The GM generates it.** The user (who will play this campaign) has explicitly requested that the canon protagonist's identity, gender, fate, and relationship to the player's game-avatar be **GM-generated and hidden from the player** until discovered in play. The user will not read the protagonist's section of the world lore file. The user will pick the protagonist's *name* (a single seeding input); everything else — what happened to them, where they are now, whether they map to the player's game-avatar, whether they are alive or dead — is generated by the GM and discovered through play.

**The mystery questions the player investigates:**
- Was the canon protagonist a man or a woman? (The game let you pick. Canon has one answer. The sages may disagree.)
- Was it *my* character — the build I played — or someone else entirely? (The game fictionalized this one figure. The player's avatar may not map cleanly to the canon figure.)
- Why does the lore get mixed up? (The sages reference a leader, but the details conflict. One sage says "he," another says "she," a third uses a title without gender. The player's game-memory has one version; canon has another; the sages' memories have five more. **This is the moment the player realizes their knowledge is partial** — but ONLY about this one figure. Everything else they remember is real.)
- Where is the protagonist now? (Alive? Dead? Missing? Hidden? One of the 9 in disguise? The 10th figure the sages erased? The 28-year-old who walked away from the war and the rule, with their own agenda?)

**The reference count:** The 5 sages reference the protagonist. If the player's villain seat is one of the sages (e.g., Seat C as the strongest sage), then only 4 *other* sages are around to reference the protagonist — the player-sage's body is one of the 5, and the player is now inside the figure the others reference. That is a different campaign than being a rebel villain.

**The two-protagonist structure (LOCKED):** The Series 1 protagonist exists in canon and is NOT the Series 2 hero. Two protagonists across two eras:
- **P1 (Series 1 protagonist):** ~28 years old now. Was 16 during the war. Led the 9 heroes. The bishop's lover (in the game; in canon — TBD by GM). Conspicuously absent from the public record. Their current agenda, location, and survival status are GM-generated.
  - **Name: Lucas Redagon.** Nickname: **Luce or Lucy** — gender-ambiguous by design. The player in Series 2 cannot tell from the nickname whether the canon protagonist was male (Luce) or female (Lucy). The sages use the nickname; the ambiguity is in-world, not just a player-side confusion. This is the protagonist mystery working in the name itself (§1.3).
- **P2 (Series 2 hero):** 22 years old. Was 10 when the war ended. Native rebel, no meta-knowledge, gathering allies to kill the sages. The player's *opponent*.
  - **Name: Samuel Astagrand.**

P1 and P2 are different people with different relationships to the sages. P1 knew the sages personally (was their leader, possibly the bishop's lover); P2 knows them only as tyrants. If they meet, they will disagree on what to do with the sages (kill? reform? spare? something else?). The player — the villain, the transmigrator — is a third figure, the wildcard in that dynamic, not a protagonist.

**The GM-generation rule:** When the engine-ready `world_lore_*.md` is generated, the protagonist's full canon entry (identity, gender, fate, relationship to the bishop, relationship to the player's game-avatar, current location and agenda) MUST be present in the lore file for the GM to consult — but the user will not read it. This is a "do not peek" sealed section. The player discovers it through play, not through pre-reading.

### 1.4 PRE-WARM-UP FEATURE NOTE (PARKED)

The first few GM messages could be a scripted on-rails sequence — the night stream, the hidden ending, the prompt, the blackout — before open play begins. The GM narrates but the player's responses are funnelled toward the awakening. Worth implementing as a campaign opener hook in the engine. Not blocking; reserved for engine integration later.

## 2. SERIES 1 ROUGHLINE (THE HISTORY)

The world the player studied. This is the game they beat.

### 2.1 The Big Bad — Orc Lord (not Demon Lord)

Deliberate choice: **no cosmic evil**. The Orc Lord is a mortal threat — a warlord, not a dark god. This grounds the world:

- Humans were enslaved by orc forces. The war was a *slavery* war, not a cosmic war.
- The 5 heroes were heroes from the human perspective. From the orc perspective, they were conquerors who broke the orc nation.
- **Demons exist as a race** — not inherently stronger or weaker than humans, orcs, elves, dwarves, beastfolk. Each race has its own advantages. No race is cosmically evil. The Demon Lord trope is deliberately refused.
- The continent is multi-racial. Series 1 was a human-vs-orc conflict *within* a multi-racial world, not a humans-vs-evil conflict.

### 2.2 The Nine Heroes

A party of nine who defeated the Orc Lord and freed humanity. Five survived to become the Five Sages; four died during the war or its immediate aftermath. The player is **not** one of the nine — in the game, the player controlled a custom-built protagonist and recruited these 9 as companions. The 9 are the canon heroes; the player's game-avatar is a separate figure whose canon status is a GM-decided mystery (§1.2).

**Why nine, not five:** The four deaths make the war real. A war where everyone survives is a fairy tale; a war where four of nine die is a history. The four deaths also seed the survivors' guilt, the hidden ending's dark secret (§2.4), and the emotional payload for Series 2 — each sage is shaped by *which* of their friends they lost and how.

**The sandbox frame:** The game was open-world, sandboxy, free-build (Skyrim-tier). Scripted events embedded in a world you could ignore. The 9 were the companion roster the player recruited across the campaign — some mandatory, some optional. The player spent hundreds of hours with them, ran their loyalty quests, saw their cutscenes, knew their kits and weaknesses. In canon, the 9 are real historical figures. The player's memories of them are a translation of a translation — close, but not identical (§1.2).

**The roster (archetypes + names pending):**

**The deaths, summarized:**
- #3 (Heroine 2) dies to a tactical mistake by one of the survivors — the guilt seed, someone's fault.

- #6 (Old man) dies heroically — the clean death, the martyr everyone honors.
- #9 (Cook/alchemist) dies after the war — assassinated, poisoned, or chose death. The death that wasn't supposed to happen. The one that broke them.

**The masked hero (#5) is the MASK SAGE — the player's landing point (§1.1.2).** A human hero who wore a mask during the war and operates behind the scenes — conveniently never in the same battle as the Series 1 protagonist, so the player's game-knowledge of them is minimal by construction. In the 12 post-war years, the masked hero became the Sage-Magus (surveillance meritocracy). The mask + voice-magic modulator conceals their identity even from P2. This is the only sage slot a transmigrator can safely inhabit, because nobody knows who's supposed to be under the mask. Beer-and-moonlight moments still happened — camaraderie doesn't require an unmasked face, and the player may have warm game-memories of a figure they never saw.

**⚠️ RESOLVED: No non-human representative in the 9.** The 9 heroes are all-human. The multi-racial continent (orcs, demons, elves, dwarves, beastfolk, etc.) still exists and the lid mechanic (§2.5) still holds — the sages' tyranny suppresses other races — but the 9 war heroes who became the sages were all human. This is cleaner and arguably strengthens the lid theme: the human sages holding down the non-human races is a clearer oppression dynamic than "one of the jailers is non-human."

**Open question for the user:** Does the death/survival split above feel right, or do you want to move someone? (The biggest remaining lever: should the best friend die instead of survive? That flips the campaign from "betrayal" to "avenge my friend by exposing the sages who sacrificed him.")

### 2.3 The War's Aftermath (the bridge into Series 2)

**Timeline: 12 years, not decades.** This is a load-bearing decision. The sages are 28 years old in Series 2, not old men — and **stronger than they were in Series 1** because they are adults.

- The Orc Lord's defeat fractured the orc nation. Remnants were pushed into borderlands.
- The Five Heroes were granted governance of the freed human territories as a reward.
- For 12 years, the survivors ruled. The heroes were legends — young legends, 16 when they won, now 28 holding thrones they grew into.
- **The sages' power curve is critical.** The player's game-memory is of *teenage* sages — teenage kits, teenage weaknesses, teenage habits, teenage emotional volatility. The adult sages have had 12 years to train, consolidate, and shore up every weakness the player remembers. The player isn't fighting the bosses they memorized; they're fighting the bosses those bosses *grew into*. Meta-knowledge is not just wrong-era, it's wrong-*version* — like knowing a Pokémon's first form and facing the evolution. Some of the player's remembered strategies will still work (the sages' core natures don't change). Many will not. The player must re-learn the adults from the teenagers they knew.
- The sages' rule hardened over the 12 years, hardening most in the first few. Young rulers with PTSD from a war where they watched 4 friends die (and chose to let it happen) do not mellow into wise monarchs. They overcompensate. They crack down. By 28, the tyranny is institutional, the justification rehearsed, the doubt buried. Each sage's region developed its own flavor of tyranny — one a military state, one a faith-state, one a surveillance meritocracy, one a border cage, one an information oligarchy.
- The common people suffer. The sages justify it as *necessary* — and they may be partly right. (See §2.5 — the lid.)
- The 9th hero's death (cook/alchemist, post-war) is the sages' founding wound — now 12 years old but never fully processed. The sages were 16-18 when it happened. Their tyranny is partly calcified grief, partly buried guilt, partly genuine conviction that mercy failed.

**Tonal consequence:** Series 2 is not "old regime crumbling" or "young regime cracking." It is **"adult regime that has had 12 years to make its tyranny look like order."** The sages at 28 are not impulsive teenagers — they are competent, institutionalized, and have had time to build systems. They are harder to kill than the player remembers, harder to argue with, and harder to dismiss as simply evil. The moral weight is heaviest when the tyrant is a 28-year-old who has had 12 years to convince themselves they were right.

### 2.4 The Hidden Ending's Dark Secret (LOCKED)

The public story: *"9 heroes fought, 4 died nobly, 5 survived to rule."*

There are **two** "true endings" in the game. The player found the second one — the one nobody found in 3 years.

**The FAKE true ending (the one most completionists find):**
*"Everyone survives."* With high enough affinity, the right gear, and the right choices, the player saves all 9 companions through the war. The credits roll. The developers put this in so players could feel good. It is a lie. It is what the audience wants history to be.

**The REAL true ending (the one our player found, the one nobody found in 3 years):**
The one where **the right people die.** The 4 deaths that canon actually had — #3 (mistake), #4 (betrayed), #6 (heroic), #9 (post-war) — happen in the right ways, at the right moments, to the right people. The player has to *choose to let them die.* Not through negligence — through the specific, correct, canon-accurate calls that result in those specific deaths. The credits roll differently. The sages' tyranny is foreshadowed in the epilogue.

**The thesis:** Real war doesn't let everyone survive. The developers encoded the actual canon history into this ending — the deaths that really happened. To reach it, the player must accept that some people die and that trying to save everyone is the coward's route. The "everyone survives" ending is the comforting fiction; the "right people die" ending is the history.

**The dark mirror (the player's original sin):**
The player already made the sages' choice. In the game, before they ever arrived in Series 2, they chose to let the 4 die — because it was the "true" ending, because it was canon, because 100,000 players missed it and they wanted to be the first. They are not innocent. They are the sages, in miniature, in the game. The campaign's thesis lands before the player even wakes up in the villain's body: *you already chose to let your friends die for the real ending. The sages did the same thing, but for real.*

This is why the sages' original sin is not a secret the player learns — it is a secret the player *already knows they share.* The horror is recognition, not discovery.

**The tell (revisited):** When the player uses Series 1 knowledge in Series 2, the most dangerous thing they know is not *that* the 4 died — everyone knows that. It's *how* and *why* they died, and that the 5 chose it. The 5 sages know they chose it. They have spent 12 years building the lie that the deaths were unavoidable — a lie repeated long enough that the sages themselves may half-believe it. The player knows it was a choice. The player *made the same choice.* That is the noose.

**This is also the seed of Seat F (fallen ally).** The hidden ending implies the dark route exists in Series 2: the Series 2 hero, facing the same kind of choice, could make the same call the 5 sages made — let friends die to win. That choice is what spawns the fallen-ally villain. The cycle repeats because the original sin was never confessed — and the player is the only person alive who knows it was a sin at all.

### 2.5 The Lid (why the sages are not simply wrong)

The sages' tyranny is also the **lid** holding down the other races and the orc remnants. Their iron rule kept the post-war peace by force. Topple a sage → the region is "freed" → the orcs rise, the demon clans maneuver, the beast territories assert themselves. The player's success as a villain *causes* the world war.

This is the campaign's core dilemma:
- Series 2 knowledge says: sages are tyrants, kill them.
- Series 1 knowledge says: sages are the only wall between humans and re-enslavement.
- Which meta-knowledge does the player trust?

## 3. SERIES 2 ROUGHLINE (THE CAMPAIGN)

### 3.1 The Villain Seat Framework (LOCKED)

Six possible insertion points along the same hero's journey. The player picks which seat at campaign meta-start. Each seat has different starting knowledge, power, danger, and relationship to the sages. **None of this is known to the player-character at wake-up** (§1.1.1 — Discovery Rule). The player discovers their seat through play.

- **Seat A — Early Villain:** A local tyrant the resistance must defeat first. Low power, low knowledge. The player is the *tutorial antagonist* of the rebellion arc. *Discovery: the player wakes up in a body that locals fear or resent; they hear rumors of a young rebel gathering allies; they realize they're the first target.*
- **Seat B — Mid Boss:** A regional threat the resistance must overcome to prove itself. Medium power. A wall. *Discovery: the player wakes up in a body with regional authority or threat; the hero's rebellion has already formed and is coming for them as the proving fight.*
- **Seat C — Final Boss:** The climax opponent. The strongest sage, or the strongest unleashed power after the sages fall. Highest power. The thesis opponent. *Discovery: the player wakes up in a body with immense power and institutional authority — possibly a sage themselves. The hero's entire arc is aimed at them. The player is the endgame.*
- **Seat D — Right Hand:** The penultimate fight. Someone personally tied to the resistance's leader (the hero of Series 2). Often more emotional than C. *Discovery: the player wakes up in a body that is close to the hero — a mentor, a family member, a former friend. They discover the hero's rebellion and realize they are the obstacle the hero must overcome before the final sage.*
- **Seat E — Secret Boss (post-game):** A threat that awakens *because* the sages fell. Optional super-threat. The consequence made flesh. *Discovery: the player wakes up sealed, dormant, or hidden. They don't know how long they've been out. The world they find is one where the sages have ALREADY fallen — the hero won — and the player is the consequence that victory unleashed.*
- **Seat F — Fallen Ally (branch only, EMERGENT):** NOT a starting condition. A villain that only exists because the hero of Series 2 made the dark-route choice during play. The player in Seat F starts as an ally (or potential ally) and BECOMES the villain through the hero's action. *Discovery: the player wakes up in a body that is one of the hero's allies or recruits. They play as a party member. At some point, the hero makes the sages' choice — sacrifices a friend (possibly the player) to win — and the player is turned. The player discovers they are the villain by being betrayed.* **The thesis seat:** power corrupts the righteous, even the rebellion's own. The cycle repeats, and the player is the evidence.

### 3.2 The Hero of Series 2 (LOCKED — native, 22 years old, rebel, the PLAYER'S OPPONENT)

**The player is NOT the hero. The player is the villain — and could be one of the sages.** The Series 2 hero is the player's *opponent*: the rebel coming to kill the sages, including (depending on seat) the player. If the player is Seat C (a sage), the player IS the boss this 22-year-old is coming to kill. The player is not on the hero's side. The player is the obstacle.

**Locked hero profile:**
- **Age:** 22 years old. An adult, not a teenager — mature gameplay.
- **War memory:** Was 10 years old when the war ended. Old enough to remember the orcs' cruelty, the fear, and the sages' *victory* — old enough to have once cheered the heroes who would become tyrants. Young enough that his entire political awakening (ages 10-22) happened under the sages' rule. The hero remembers when the sages were heroes; he grew up watching them stop being heroes. That is psychologically richer than pure hatred — the hero's grudge is compounded by the betrayal of his childhood idols.
- **Origin:** One of the oppressed. Not a chosen one, not a noble in disguise — someone who suffered under the sages' rule and decided to fight back.
- **Goal:** Gather allies, kill all 5 sages, free the world from their tyranny. Simple, direct, revolutionary. The player's opponent is a 22-year-old revolutionary with a knife, a grudge, and a childhood memory of the people he's now hunting.
- **Knowledge:** The hero has NO Series 1 meta-knowledge. The hero knows only the canon history — the public story, the sages' rule, the suffering. The hero does not know the hidden ending's secret. The hero does not know the sages chose to let the 4 die. The hero just knows the sages are tyrants and the world bleeds.

**The player's relationship to this hero defines the campaign — and depends entirely on which villain seat the player picked:**

- If the player is Seat A/B: the hero kills you early. Your campaign is short and tragic unless you escape or convert.
- If the player is Seat C: the hero is your nemesis. The campaign is the slow dance toward the final battle.
- If the player is Seat D: the hero is your former friend/lover/child/student. The penultimate fight is personal.
- If the player is Seat E: the hero has already won, and you are the consequence. The campaign is post-victory.
- If the player is Seat F: the hero *made* you. You are their sin — the ally they corrupted with the same choice the 5 sages made.

**The hero is native. The player is the only foreigner.** This keeps the world grounded and makes the player's Series 1 knowledge the sole source of foreignness — not a second isekai arrival, not a parallel transmigrator. The mirror between player and hero is *fan meets idol* (the player admired the sages; the hero is fighting them) and *foreigner meets native* (the player knows things; the hero is of the place). No co-participation, no shared origin.

### 3.3 The Climax — What Is Seat C?

The sages are **mid-game villains**, not the final boss. The true climax is the world war their fall unleashes. So Seat C is *not* a sage. Candidates:

- **The Series 1 protagonist returned** — P1, the 10th figure, now ~28, with their own agenda. They walked away from the war and the rule; they have been absent for 12 years; they come back when the sages start falling. Their relationship to the bishop, to the sages, and to the player (whose game-avatar they may or may not be) makes them the most personally loaded climax opponent. **Strongest candidate given §1.2–§1.3.** The player's most intimate mystery becomes the final boss.
- The strongest of the unleashed races (orc new lord, demon clan king — risks retreading Series 1)
- A **6th sage** nobody remembers — but since the 4 deaths are real (§1.2), this would have to be a figure who was never one of the 9, not a "dead" hero who survived. Weaker.
- The world war itself has no single boss — it's a multi-faction endgame, and C is whichever faction the player chose *not* to stop.

**Note:** The "one of the 4 dead actually survived" twist is OFF the table — the 4 deaths are canon and real. The mystery is concentrated in P1, not in the dead.

## 5. TONE & SETTING ANCHORS

- **Genre:** Medieval fantasy, post-victory. Grounded, not cosmically epic. The Orc Lord was a warlord, not a dark god.
- **Magitech ceiling:** Domestic utility magic (lamps, plumbing, carriage-lights, message crystals). No industrial magitech, no magitech armies. Society is still feudal.
- **Tone:** EPIC-intimate. The wars of great figures rendered in the mud they march through. The cost of every order measured in peasants. (Borrowed from Three Kingdoms starter tone — fits the post-sage collapse.)
- **Races:** Multi-racial continent. Each race has advantages. No race is cosmically evil. Demons are a race, not a faction of evil.
- **The inversion:** The "villains" may be the righteous. The "heroes" became the tyrants. The cycle is the antagonist.
