# Three Kingdoms — War of the Mandate

## 1. WORLD OVERVIEW

### OVERVIEW — Core Premise
This campaign begins in **178 AD**, the last quiet year of the Han. The empire is corrupt, the court is a knife-fight of eunuchs, the peasants are already hungry — but the war has not started. **Zhang Jiao** is a wandering Daoist faith-healer gathering followers in the northeast, and in the summer of **179 AD** he will climb Mount Taiqing with his two brothers and perform the **Rite of the Great Peace** — a ritual meant to end sickness. **The Rite will work.** From that night onward, no one within the reach of the Han world will die of old age. The body stops aging at whatever year it had reached. A man of forty stays forty. A woman of sixty stays sixty. The old stay old — they do not rejuvenate, but they do not decline. The only doors out of the world are now **violence and disease** — and the diseases are fewer. The population that should have thinned itself naturally over a generation will, instead, keep growing. The grain will not.

The party may witness the Rite. They may try to stop it. They may try to help it. They may not be in the northeast at all. **What they do from 178 onward is the campaign.** The history below is the shape of what *will* happen if the party does not intervene — the default track, the course the river takes when no one dams it.

**The default course (the history the party may diverge from):**
The Han court, terrified of a faith-healer more beloved than the emperor, moves to arrest Zhang Jiao in 184. The arrest becomes the **Yellow Turban Rebellion**, the rebellion becomes a massacre, and Zhang Jiao dies of a plague-sore during the campaign (disease still takes the careless). His brothers are executed. But the **Rite holds**. The Great Peace is still in effect. The Han collapses anyway — the eunuchs, Dong Zhuo, the burning of Luoyang, the warlords — and the three men who carve up the corpse of the Han are all, still, alive decades later, because none of them can age past the year they were at when the Rite caught them.

**Cao Cao** is forty-five and will be forty-five forever. **Liu Bei** is twenty-eight and will be twenty-eight forever. **Sun Quan** — born after the Rite — stops at twenty-one. **Zhuge Liang** stops at twenty-seven. The founding generation does not die of years. The second generation grows up beside them and cannot push them off the stage. **Cao Pi** waits for a father who will never weaken. **Liu Shan** is a man of fifty in the body of a boy. The war grinds on for **three generations** and there is no natural mechanism for it to end — because the men running it will not grow old, and the only thing that kills them is each other.

The veil between the mortal and the uncanny is thin, and the Rite of Great Peace is the wound it will open. Daoist hermits can sometimes call the wind. Yellow Turban sorcerers will curse granaries in the name of the Way of Great Peace. A horse named Red Hare can outrun any man alive. A spear named Sky Piercer has never met an armor it could not break. These things are **rare, named, and feared** — not a magic system, but omens and heirlooms that surface only at the hinge-points of history. Most soldiers still die of dysentery, arrow-wounds, and hunger, same as they always have — the Rite does not save them from those.

The campaign is not about the warlords. It is about the people the warlords step on: the conscript dragged out of his field at harvest-time, the village that feeds an army for one night and starves for three winters, the refugee column strafed by "friendly" cavalry, the magistrate who must choose between his lord's grain quota and his people surviving the snow. The Mandate is contested in the high halls; the Mandate is **paid for** in the mud. And if no one diverges the river, the mud will be **three generations deep** — because the warlords will not die, the war will not end, and the peasants are the only people in this world who still do.

**Tone:** GRIM, HISTORICAL, EPIC-INTIMATE, WAR-REALISM, OCCULT-ADJACENT
**Core Theme:** The cost of immortality, measured in peasants. The warlords cannot age; the war cannot end; the only people who still die of years are the ones the warlords step on.
**Campaign Start:** 178 AD — the last year before the Rite. The party may be Han officials, northeast villagers, wandering soldiers, Yellow Turban sympathizers, or whatever else the GM and players decide. The Rite is about to happen. Nothing is fixed.
**Default End (if the party does not diverge):** ~265 AD — Sima Yi's clan completes its long usurpation and founds the Jin dynasty. The ageless founders are, one by one, killed by each other or by the Sima apparatus; the survivors are absorbed. The war "ends." The peasants are still starving. This is the historical track, and it is the track the party is here to break.
**Timeline Note:** An alt-history from 179 AD onward. The Rite of Great Peace halts senescence across the Han world; violence and disease remain the only causes of death. This allows the full *Dynasty Warriors* roster — founders, sons, and grandsons — to coexist without anachronism. **Dead is dead** — historical deaths (Dian Wei at Wancheng 197, Zhang Jiao of plague 184, Taishi Ci of a siege wound 206, etc.) stand as the default fate unless the party's actions change them. **The old stay old** — the Rite freezes you at your current age; it does not rejuvenate. Huang Zhong was already elderly in 179 and remains elderly, fighting from the body of a white-bearded sixty-year-old who will never grow frailer but will never grow younger either. The GM may run any campaign year from 178 to 265 without breaking the cast; beyond 265, see the **Warriors Orochi continuation note** below.
**Warriors Orochi Continuation Note:** If the campaign reaches the Sima Jin endpoint (or any late-era endpoint the party drives the world to), the engine may hand off to a **Warriors Orochi crossover campaign** as a continuation mode. That campaign — in which the ageless survivors of the Three Kingdoms era are abducted into the serpent-god Orochi's fused dimension alongside warriors from other worlds — is a separate lore file and should be built only after this campaign's content is settled. The Rite of Great Peace's long shadow (ageless warlords, an unending war, a peasantry that has been dying for a century) gives the Orochi crossover a uniquely heavy cast of survivors to draw on. Leave this as a seed; do not develop it here.

**Culture & Starter Note:** This is **ancient China** — late Eastern Han through early Jin, with the *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* layer of embellishment. Social norms are period-appropriate: filial piety, ancestor veneration, courtesy-names, court ritual, the supremacy of grain, the shame of a daughter sold, the glory of a son who passes the court exam. The GM should enforce the *feel* of the culture without being pedantic about exact dates.
**Player starters — pick one at campaign start:**
- **Native:** A subject of the Han — a farmer's child, a minor clerk, a frontier soldier, a Daoist novice, a merchant's heir. You know this world. You are of it. Your stakes are personal.
- **Isekai:** A modern person pulled across at the moment of the Rite (179 AD) — you arrive with nothing but your wits and whatever the Rite's wake did to you. You do not know the customs, and you are about to discover that the history you half-remember from video games is now a place you are standing in. The GM should let the culture shock be real and let the player's out-of-world knowledge be useful *sometimes* and dangerously wrong *often*.
The engine should treat both starters as valid and should not privilege one over the other. The campaign's question — who will end the Rite, and why — is the same either way.

## 2. FACTIONS

### FACTION — Cao Wei (The Northern Court)
**Type:** Warlord State / Centralizing Bureaucracy
**Key Members:** Cao Cao (Lord Chancellor), Cao Pi (heir), Sima Yi (strategist), Xiahou Dun (general)
**Stance:** Pro-establishment (claims the legitimate succession of Han).
Cao Cao holds the emperor at Xuchang and rules in the Son of Heaven's name. The Wei apparatus is the most modern state in the land: the **tuntian** military-farm system feeds its armies off conscripted labor, the court is staffed by meritocratic promotion, and Wei law is harsh but consistent. To Wei, the other two kingdoms are rebels against the rightful throne. Its armies are vast, well-supplied, and endlessly reinforced by the levée — every northern village owes Wei a quota of sons. The price of Wei's order is that the village cannot refuse.

### FACTION — Shu Han (The Legitimist Remnant)
**Type:** Warlord State / Claimant Dynasty
**Key Members:** Liu Bei (emperor-claimant), Zhuge Liang (chancellor-strategist), Guan Yu, Zhang Fei, Zhao Yun
**Stance:** Hostile to Wei, claims the true Han lineage through Liu Bei's distant royal blood.
Shu is the smallest and poorest of the three, squeezed into the mountainous Yi province (modern Sichuan). It survives on the moral legitimacy of Liu Bei's reputation for benevolence — and on Zhuge Liang's administrative genius and eerie tactical reputation. Shu's cause is romantic; Shu's logistics are desperate. Every northern campaign Zhuge Liang leads drains Yi of grain and men it cannot spare, and the peasants of Yi die in the mountain passes for a dynasty they will never see. Shu tells itself it fights for the people. The people's opinion is not recorded.

### FACTION — Eastern Wu (The Riverlord State)
**Type:** Warlord State / Riverine Hegemony
**Key Members:** Sun Quan (lord of Wu), Zhou Yu (strategist, d. 210), Lu Su, Gan Ning, Lu Xun
**Stance:** Defensive hegemon, formally allied with Shu against Wei.
Wu controls the Yangtze and the rich rice-lands south of it. Its strength is its navy, its wealth, and the natural wall of the great river. Wu is the least zealous of the three — it would rather hold what it has than chase the Mandate. But Wu's lords are aristocratic families who squeeze their tenant-farmers hard, and Sun Quan's alliances shift with the wind. A village in Wu pays fewer conscripts than a village in Wei, but it pays more in rice-tax, and the tax-collector is just as willing to flog the slow.

### FACTION — The Yellow Turban Remnants
**Type:** Daoist Insurrection / Heretical Cult
**Key Members:** Zhang Jiao (founder, deceased), surviving "Way of Great Peace" sect leaders scattered across Qing and Ji provinces
**Stance:** Hostile to all three kingdoms; apocalyptic.
The great rebellion of 184 AD was crushed, but its cells were never fully burned out — and its founder's **Rite** is still in effect, which means the Turbans are the only faction that can plausibly claim to know how the agelessness began and how it might end. In the famine-belt of the northeast, scattered Turban preachers still move among the starving peasants, promising that the "Blue Sky" will fall and a "Yellow Heaven" will rise in its place. Their sorcery — granary-curses, spirit-possession rites, omen-reading — is the only true magic the common folk ever see, and it is the surviving fragment of the corpus that performed the Rite of Great Peace. The Turbans feed on the war's wreckage: every burned village is a recruitment sermon, and every ageless peasant who has watched the warlords refuse to die for forty years is a ready convert. All three kingdoms hunt them, and all three kingdoms secretly fear them — because the Turbans offer the peasant something none of the warlords do: a story in which the peasant is not the one who dies, and a promise that the warlords, finally, can.

### FACTION — The Way of the Celestial Masters (Orthodox Daoism)
**Type:** Religious Organization / Neutral Power
**Key Members:** Zhang Lu (the "Teacher" / Celestial Master, ruling Hanzhong)
**Stance:** Neutral, sanctuary-offering, distrusted by all three courts.
Based in the mountain fastness of Hanzhong, the Celestial Masters run a theocratic mini-state that offers grain, refuge, and ritual purity to any who flee the war. They are the orthodox cousins of the Yellow Turbans and despise them as heretics, but to the warlords the distinction is academic. Zhang Lu's domain is one of the few places a deserter or a starving family can find safety — which is exactly why Wei, Shu, and Wu all want it. Its existence is a quiet reproach to every army that marches past it.

### FACTION — The Xiongnu and Wuhuan Northern Allies
**Type:** Nomadic Confederacy / Mercenary Cavalry
**Key Members:** Chief Kebineng (Xianbei), various Wuhuan chieftains
**Stance:** Opportunistic, nominally allied to Wei, paid in grain and captives.
The northern steppe peoples have watched the Han collapse with patient hunger. Cao Cao has bought many of them with grain, titles, and the right to take captives from Wei's own frontier villages in exchange for cavalry service. The alliance is a quiet scandal: Wei's frontier peasants are sometimes raided by the very horsemen Wei's treasury pays. This is known in the court. It is not known in the villages, because the villages are gone.

### FACTION — The Refugee Columns (The Liuliang)
**Type:** Stateless mass / informal network
**Key Members:** No leaders — only headmen, aunties, and whoever still has a cart
**Stance:** Surviving; allied to no one, preyed on by all.
The great unspoken faction. Hundreds of thousands of displaced villagers stream along the roads, fleeing armies that took their sons and granaries that took their seed-grain. They are not an army, they are not a cause. They are the demographic wound the war has opened. Every kingdom's foragers will take from them; every kingdom's press-gangs will lift men out of them; every kingdom's quartermasters will log them as "bandits" when they resist. They are the people the campaign is *about*.

## 3. LOCATIONS

### LOCATION — Xuchang (The Puppet Capital)
**Type:** Imperial Capital (de facto)
**Status:** Flourishing (uneasy)
The seat of the captive Han emperor and the heart of Cao Cao's administration. Xuchang is clean, ordered, and full of informants. The court pays its functionaries on time, the granaries are full, and the streets are swept. This prosperity is bought with the grain of the tuntian farms and the silence of the censor's office. A visitor from the ravaged north will find Xuchang almost obscene in its calm.

### LOCATION — The Yellow River Plain (Northern War Belt)
**Type:** Breadbasket / War Zone
**Status:** Devastated, repeatedly contested
The great alluvial plain between the Yellow and Huai rivers — theoretically the richest farmland under heaven, in practice the most trampled. Every campaign season the armies cross it; every crossing burns the year's crop. Villages here are rebuilt three times in a generation. The population is a fraction of what it was under the Han. Whole townships exist only as a name on a tax-roll with no one left to pay it. This is where the campaign's "civilian hardship" lives closest to the surface.

### LOCATION — Jiangling and the Middle Yangtze
**Type:** Strategic River City / contested
**Status:** Contested (Wei and Wu clash here)
The hinge of the south. Whoever holds Jiangling holds the movement of grain and troops between north and south. It has changed hands four times in eight years. Its civilian population has largely fled; what remains is a garrison town, a river-port, and a market for war-profiteers selling stolen grain at famine prices.

### LOCATION — Chengdu (Shu's Mountain Capital)
**Type:** Provincial Capital / Isolated Stronghold
**Status:** Flourishing (insular)
Deep in the Yi province basin, ringed by mountains that cost more men to cross than any battle. Chengdu is warm, well-fed, and culturally rich — and quietly resentful of the constant drain of its sons northward for Zhuge Liang's expeditions. A peasant in Chengdu may never see a Wei soldier, but he will see his cousin leave and not come back.

### LOCATION — Chaisang / Jianye (Wu's River Capital)
**Type:** Naval Capital / Aristocratic Court
**Status:** Flourishing
Sun Quan's seat on the lower Yangtze. Wealthy, river-bound, defended by the greatest fleet in the world. The court is dominated by great southern families who quarrel over policy while their tenant-farmers quietly starve in the back-parishes. The city itself is one of the few places a refugee might find work — as a rower on a warship, if he is lucky.

### LOCATION — The Red Cliffs (Chibi)
**Type:** River Narrows / Battlefield-to-be
**Status:** Impending
The bend in the Yangtze where, within the year, Cao Cao's vast northern fleet will meet the allied Wu-Shu force. This is the campaign's ticking clock. The riverbank camps are already swelling with conscripts and laborers; the local fishing villages have been requisitioned into nonexistence. Whatever happens at the Cliffs, the people of the surrounding country have already lost.

### LOCATION — Hanzhong (The Sanctuary)
**Type:** Mountain Theocracy / Neutral Zone
**Status:** Strained, overcrowded
Zhang Lu's domain — the only place in China where a deserter can buy grain without being pressed back into a banner. It is filling past capacity with refugees the Celestial Masters cannot quite feed. The warlords circle it. It will not stay neutral forever.

### LOCATION — The Liuliang Roads
**Type:** Refugee Migration Routes
**Status:** Constant, unrecorded
Not a place, a network — the strings of carts, ox-carts, and barefoot columns that trace the roads between fallen villages and any city with grain. They have no map because they move. They are robbed by bandits, press-ganged by armies, and occasionally — briefly — sheltered by a monastery or a merciful magistrate. The player party will encounter them constantly.

## 4. CHARACTERS

### CHARACTER — Cao Cao
**Aliases:** Mengde, the Lord Chancellor, the "White-Faced Tyrant" (southron slander)
**Appearance:** A lean, sharp-featured man of middle height, always precisely groomed. Short black beard trimmed to a point, piercing eyes, a thin smile that rarely reaches them. Wears dark court robes of unremarkable cut, but always immaculate. Favors a simple iron sword at his hip — never a ceremonial blade.
**Disposition:** Pragmatic, ironic, merciless, cultured.
**Personality:** Cao Cao is a poet who writes elegies for soldiers he has just ordered to die. He is genuinely superstitious and consults omens before campaigns, but never lets an omen override a supply report. He distrusts heroism and rewards competence. He will execute a lifelong friend for embezzling grain and weep at the funeral. He is funny, often, and the humor is the coldest thing about him.
**Voice:** Quick, witty, literary. Quotes the classics mid-sentence to make a cruel point. Alternates between warm banter and an absolute flatness that means someone is about to die.
**Status:** Alive
**Faction:** Cao Wei
**Goals:** To reunify the realm under a restored (and compliant) Han — and to be the one holding the emperor when it happens.
**StoryRelevance:** The dominant warlord of the age. His supply lines, his tuntian farms, and his conscription quotas are the engine of the northern civilian suffering. He is also, inconveniently, often right.
**Example Output:** "I would rather betray the world than let the world betray me. But do not mistake that for a confession — it is a warning."
**Affinity:** 35
**PersonalityHex:** drive:+3, diligence:+2, boldness:+3, warmth:-1, empathy:-1, composure:+2
**Traits:** [ambitious, proud, scheming, pragmatic, ruthless]
**SignatureEquipment:** [Qinggang Sword (an iron straight-sword said to cut through any helm), a jade tally of the Lord Chancellor's office]
**Element:** command
A warlord who has read too much history to believe in virtue, and not enough to stop trying to write his own name into it.

**Location: Xuchang**
**Intro Boost: court, chancellor, Wei, edict, Xuchang**

### CHARACTER — Liu Bei
**Aliases:** Xuande, Imperial Uncle (self-styled), the Sandal-Weaver
**Appearance:** A tall, broad man with long arms and prominent ears — a peasant's joke of a face on a lord's bearing. Bearded, slow-moving, with large expressive eyes that well up easily in public. Wears plain dark robes, often patched, and walks barefoot at ceremonies of mourning.
**Disposition:** Earnest, patient, morally agonized, charismatic.
**Personality:** Liu Bei is the most sincere hypocrite of his age — and possibly the most sincere man of his age, which is the problem. He genuinely grieves for the common people and genuinely believes the Han must be restored through him. He also knows his legitimacy rests on that grief and cannot always tell where the feeling ends and the performance begins. He weeps real tears at real funerals and also at politically useful ones.
**Voice:** Warm, slow, humble to the point of being passive-aggressive. Speaks of "the people" constantly. Long, sighing pauses. Never issues a direct order when a sorrowful hint will do.
**Status:** Alive
**Faction:** Shu Han
**Goals:** To restore the Han through his own line — and to be able to look at himself in the mirror afterwards.
**StoryRelevance:** The moral mirror the campaign holds up to the player. His cause is just; his cause also kills the Yi peasantry in the mountain passes.
**Example Output:** "If I took this province by force, the people would suffer. If I do not take it, the people will suffer under Cao Cao. Tell me, friend — which sin may I weep for first?"
**Affinity:** 60
**PersonalityHex:** drive:+3, diligence:+2, boldness:+1, warmth:+3, empathy:+3, composure:+1
**Traits:** [loyal, honorable, proud, protective, generous]
**SignatureEquipment:** [a pair of iron shuanggu swords (the "paired husband-and-wife blades"), a tattered sandal he keeps as a memento of his weaving youth]
**Element:** virtue
A man whose goodness is real and whose goodness is also his propaganda.

**Wandering: true**
**Intro Boost: Han, the people, Yi, benevolence, refugee**

### CHARACTER — Zhuge Liang
**Aliases:** Kongming, the Sleeping Dragon, the Chancellor
**Appearance:** A tall, thin scholar in his late twenties, with a pale, indoor complexion and a long, careful beard. Always carries a feather fan — crane feathers, white — which he uses to gesture, to hide his mouth when thinking, and occasionally to buy himself one more second before answering. Plain white Daoist-adjacent robes.
**Disposition:** Calm, precise, ironic, quietly tired.
**Personality:** The most intelligent man of his generation, and he knows it, which is the flaw. Zhuge Liang has read every available text on governance, agriculture, and war, and he runs Shu's state as a solved problem. The unsolved part — that the peasants still die, that the expeditions still fail, that the Mandate still does not come — is the part he cannot sleep over. He believes in Liu Bei's cause more than Liu Bei does. He is beginning to suspect it is not enough.
**Voice:** Unhurried, measured, fond of analogies drawn from farming and chess. Never raises his voice. Uses the word "perhaps" as a weapon.
**Status:** Alive
**Faction:** Shu Han
**Goals:** To restore the Han through Shu — and, privately, to prove that virtue and competence together can still win, against all evidence.
**StoryRelevance:** The strategist behind Shu's campaigns and the architect of the mountain logistics that bleed Yi. He is also the closest thing the setting has to a "real" magician — whether his weather-rites work or he simply reads the sky better than anyone alive is a question the campaign should never quite answer.
**Example Output:** "The wind favors the patient, my lord. Whether it favors the righteous, I have not yet determined. Perhaps we should prepare the boats regardless."
**Affinity:** 55
**PersonalityHex:** drive:+2, diligence:+3, boldness:0, warmth:0, empathy:+2, composure:+3
**Traits:** [loyal, honorable, proud, pragmatic, secretive]
**SignatureEquipment:** [a white crane-feather fan, a set of silk tactical maps of the mountain passes]
**SignatureAbilities:** [weather-reading (omen-grade, never openly confirmed as sorcery), the "Empty Fort" psychological ruse]
**Element:** wind
A genius who has begun to suspect that genius is not the same as virtue.

**Location: Chengdu (but travels with Shu campaigns)**
**Intro Boost: stratagem, wind, fog, expedition, Shu, dragon**

### CHARACTER — Sun Quan
**Aliases:** Zhongmou, the Young Lord of the East, the River-King
**Appearance:** A compact, athletic man in his mid-twenties with a square jaw, short beard, and unusually broad, strong shoulders from a youth spent rowing. Dresses in river-blue silks of obvious quality but never ostentation. Eyes that watch a room before he enters it.
**Disposition:** Cautious, proud, clan-minded, slow to commit.
**Personality:** Sun Quan inherited the east young and has spent his reign being pulled between his older, brilliant advisors (Zhou Yu, then Lu Su) and his great aristocratic families who want peace at any price. He is a better administrator than a general and he knows it. He would rather hold the Yangtze for fifty years than cross it once. He is not cruel, but he is patient, and patience in a warlord is its own kind of cruelty to the people waiting for the war to end.
**Voice:** Measured, courtly, with a southerner's drawl that northerners underestimate. Asks questions. Lots of questions. The questions are the verdict.
**Status:** Alive
**Faction:** Eastern Wu
**Goals:** To keep Wu intact, wealthy, and dynastically marriageable — the Mandate can wait until the other two bleed each other dry.
**StoryRelevance:** The third corner of the triangle. His decisions at Red Cliffs will shape the campaign's climax, and his tenant-farmers are quietly the best-fed peasants in China — which is a very low bar.
**Example Output:** "My uncle took this river. My brother held it. I am not going to be the Sun who lost it. Make me an argument that does not require me to risk it."
**Affinity:** 50
**PersonalityHex:** drive:+2, diligence:+2, boldness:-1, warmth:+1, empathy:+1, composure:+2
**Traits:** [proud, protective, stubborn, pragmatic, territorial]
**SignatureEquipment:** [a Wu-made naval officer's saber, a jade tally of the Sun clan]
**Element:** river
A lord whose caution is his virtue and his cage.

**Location: Chaisang / Jianye**
**Intro Boost: Wu, Yangtze, river, fleet, Sun**

### CHARACTER — Old Mother Geng (the Refugee Headwoman)
**Aliases:** Geng Po, "the old crow" (by her own grandchildren)
**Appearance:** A small, sun-blackened woman in her sixties, missing three teeth and half of her left ear (frostbite, two winters ago). Grey hair bound in a working-cloth. Wears four layers of patched hemp, none of them matching. Carries a bamboo staff she does not need but uses to hit people who waste grain.
**Disposition:** Bitter, practical, fiercely protective, dryly funny.
**Personality:** Geng Po was the wife of a village headman near Dongjun. Her village was requisitioned by a Wei foraging party in 204, then burned by a Shu raiding column in 206 for having fed Wei. She has led a refugee column of two hundred souls south for three years. She has no politics and no patience for politics. She has opinions about grain, about foot-wraps, about which warlords will press-gang a boy of fourteen and which will wait until fifteen. She remembers every name of every person she has buried.
**Voice:** Flat, regional northern accent, full of peasant proverbs. Short sentences. Swears by the names of local earth-gods no one in the south has heard of.
**Status:** Alive
**Faction:** The Refugee Columns (Liuliang)
**Goals:** To get her people to somewhere with grain and no army — ideally Hanzhong, possibly the south, certainly nowhere near a banner.
**StoryRelevance:** The heart of the campaign. Geng Po is the civilian cost of the war made articulate. Every encounter with her should make the warlords' abstractions feel like the abstractions they are.
**Example Output:** "You speak of the Mandate of Heaven. My grandson ate bark last winter. Tell me, lord — does the Mandate taste like elm?"
**Affinity:** 45 (wary of anyone armed)
**PersonalityHex:** drive:+2, diligence:+2, boldness:+1, warmth:-1, empathy:+2, composure:+1
**Traits:** [protective, stubborn, generous, pragmatic, mistrustful]

A woman the histories will not record, carrying two hundred people the histories will not record, toward a place the histories will not record.

**Wandering: true**
**Intro Boost: refugee, column, cart, road, grain, village, conscript**

### CHARACTER — Chen Shou (the Press-Ganged Conscript)
**Aliases:** "Shou" (only his mother called him Chen Shou), "the new spear"
**Appearance:** A wiry, sunburnt young man of nineteen, built like a man who has spent ten years bending over a rice paddy. Calloused hands, a missing left small finger (a childhood axe-slip), cheap homespun dyed the dull red of a Shu levy-band. Holds his spear like a man holding a spear for the third time in his life, which is to say wrong.
**Disposition:** Terrified, homesick, doggedly obedient, quietly furious.
**Personality:** Shou was taken from his father's field three weeks ago by a Shu conscription party. He does not know which way home is. He does not know why Shu fights Wei. He knows his father is too old to harvest the winter wheat alone and that his sister will have to. He is not a coward — he will stand in the line if ordered — but he is not a soldier, and everyone including him knows it. He is the median man of the campaign.
**Voice:** Rural Sichuan dialect, hesitant around officers, fluent only when complaining quietly to other conscripts. Says "yes, lord" a lot.
**Status:** Alive
**Faction:** Shu Han (conscript, 3rd month of service)
**Goals:** To survive, to go home, to harvest the wheat — in roughly that order, and he is realistic about the odds.
**StoryRelevance:** The face of the levée. Shou is what every warlord's army is actually made of. He is not a hero; he is a man with a spear he did not ask for, standing where he was told to stand.
**Example Output:** "The lord says we march north at dawn. The lord has boots. I have these." (holds up straw sandals, half-rotted) "Yes, lord. Dawn."
**Affinity:** 40
**PersonalityHex:** drive:-1, diligence:+1, boldness:-2, warmth:+1, empathy:+1, composure:-1
**Traits:** [cowardly, protective, stubborn, mistrustful]

A man the war was not for, dying in a war that was not his.

**Wandering: true**
**Intro Boost: conscript, levy, spear, Shu, march, line**

### CHARACTER — Gan Ning (the River Bandit-Captain)

## 5. POWER SYSTEM & RULES

### POWER_SYSTEM — The Rite of Great Peace and the Veil Between
There is no magic system in Three Kingdoms China. There is, however, **one great magical fact** — the Rite of Great Peace — and a handful of smaller, named uncanny things that flow from the wound the Rite tore in the world. The engine should treat all of these as *unique narrative devices*, never as mechanics, and never as something a player can learn.

**The Great Peace (the central magical fact):**
In 179 AD, Zhang Jiao and his brothers performed a rite on Mount Taiqing that was meant to end sickness. It ended the worst of it. From that night to this, **no one within the Han world dies of old age.** The body halts at whatever year it had reached. A man of forty stays forty. A woman of sixty stays sixty. Children still grow to adulthood and then stop. The only doors out of the world are now **violence and disease** — and the diseases are fewer (plague-sore, consumption, and the camp-fevers still take the careless and the poor; the well-fed and the sheltered rarely sicken). The Rite cannot be reproduced. Zhang Jiao is dead. No one knows how to end it, and the three warlords who profit from it have no incentive to try. The peasants, who bear the Malthusian cost of an ageless population on ever-shrinking arable land, pray for someone to end it daily. No one listens.

**Categories of the lesser uncanny (all downstream of the Rite):**

1. **Heirloom Artifacts** — named weapons and mounts that have passed between warlords and that are, in a word the texts avoid, *lucky*. Red Hare cannot be outrun. The Green Dragon Crescent Blade has never chipped. The Sky Piercer (Zhang Fei's spear) has broken every armor it has met. These are not +1 swords; they are objects with reputations so heavy they have become, in some unclear way, *real*. They do not glow. They do not speak. They simply always do the thing they are said to do. If a player wields one, the engine should treat it as a narrative guarantee, not a stat bonus.

2. **Daoist Omen-Reading** — Zhuge Liang, Zhang Lu, and a handful of hermits can sometimes read the sky, the wind, the ash, the birds, and be *right* in a way that exceeds meteorology. Whether this is sorcery or the last surviving body of pre-Han natural philosophy is a question the campaign must never definitively answer. It works *enough* that generals consult them. It fails *enough* that it is not a reliable weapon.

3. **Yellow Turban Curses** — the surviving Turban cells preserve a handful of village-level rites that trace back to Zhang Jiao's original corpus: the "Yellow Rot" (a granary-spoiling rite requiring ash from a previously burned store), the "Spirit-Binding" (a short-term possession rite that leaves the medium brain-damaged), and omen-reading of a darker, more political cast. These are the only openly "magical" acts in the setting, and they are performed by starving peasants, not by wizards. They are feared because they are *class warfare* as much as sorcery — and because everyone remembers that the last Turban rite actually worked.

4. **Omens and the Mandate** — the world itself is read for signs. A comet is a comment on legitimacy. A drought is a judgment. A horse born with one eye is a warning about a specific minister. The warlords believe this. The peasants believe this. The campaign should treat omens as *real enough to act on* and never confirm whether they are real.

**Hard rules for the engine:**
- No player character should ever be able to *learn* a spell or reproduce the Rite. Magic is not learned. It is inherited, cursed, or granted by a threadbare fate. The Rite is a one-time historical event; its only living inheritors are the Yellow Turban cells, and they do not fully understand what they are preserving.
- No artifact should ever be mass-produced. Each named heirloom is unique and its loss or theft is a campaign event.
- Any sorcery that appears should cost something visible — a medium's memory, a granary's worth of ash, a lifetime of a hermit's seclusion. There are no free works.
- The agelessness is **not** a power. It is a condition. No one chose it, no one controls it, and the peasant who has been twenty for forty years is not a hero — he is a man who has been pressed into six different banners and has watched his village starve six times.
- The default explanation for any "magic" event is *coincidence plus reputation plus the Rite's long shadow*. Let the players decide what they believe.

## 6. ECONOMY

### ECONOMY — Grain, Copper, and the Quota
The economy of Three Kingdoms China is, for the peasant, an economy of **grain**. Cash exists — the wuzhu copper coin is still minted, and silk is used as high-denomination currency — but the peasant pays and is paid in **shi** of grain. A shi is roughly 30 liters / 20 kg of millet or rice. A peasant family's annual tax in a "good" year is 50-60 shi; in a war year, a Wei county's quota can be 200+ shi from families that have, on hand, perhaps 40.

**The three currencies:**
- **Grain (shi)** — the real currency. Paid as tax, paid as wages to soldiers, paid as bribes, paid as tolls to pass a checkpoint. A party's wealth is best tracked in shi, not coins.
- **Copper (wuzhu / qian)** — used for small purchases in markets — a bowl of noodles, a pair of sandals, a cheap spear-head. Inflation is ruinous: a qian buys less every campaign season.
- **Silk (bolt)** — high-denomination currency for officials, warlords, and major bribes. One bolt of good silk = a junior officer's annual pay.

**The costs that matter:**
- A **conscript's spear** costs 200 qian or 1 shi. A village is issued them by the lord and billed for them.
- A **horse** costs 40-80 shi in grain, which is why cavalry are aristocrats or steppe-mercenaries, never peasants.
- A **bowl of noodles** in a garrison town costs 5-20 qian depending on how close the army is.
- A **bribe to a press-gang officer** to look the other way for one son costs 10-20 shi — a family's entire winter reserve.
- A **bribe to a magistrate** to list a son as "dead" on the rolls (so he is not conscripted) costs 30+ shi — often impossible, often attempted.

**The war economy:**
- All three kingdoms run on **requisition**. An army marching through a village takes its grain, its carts, its oxen, and its young men. A receipt is sometimes issued. The receipt is theoretically redeemable at the lord's treasury. The treasury is empty. The receipt is a piece of paper.
- **Tuntian** (Wei's military-farm system) is the most organized: soldier-farmers grow grain on seized land, half goes to the army. It works. It also means the land was seized from someone.
- **Famine prices** in a war zone: 1 shi of grain can cost 10,000 qian in a bad month — ten times the peace price. A village that eats its seed-grain in winter does not plant in spring.

## 7. EVENTS

### EVENT — The Rite of Great Peace (179 AD)
The single most important event of the age, and the one the official histories refuse to explain. Zhang Jiao and his brothers climbed Mount Taiqing and performed a rite meant to end sickness. It ended the worst of it: from that night, no one within the Han world has died of old age. The body halts at whatever year it had reached. The only doors out of the world are now violence and disease. The Rite is the campaign's original miracle and its original curse — the reason the warlords cannot die, the reason the war cannot end, and the reason the peasants are the only people in this world who still starve in any numbers that matter. The Rite cannot be reproduced. Zhang Jiao is dead. No one knows how to end it.

### EVENT — The Yellow Turban Rebellion (184 AD) and its Aftermath
Five years after the Rite, the Han court — terrified of a faith-healer more beloved than the emperor — moved to arrest Zhang Jiao. The arrest became a rebellion, the rebellion became a massacre, and the massacre became the northeast's permanent famine-belt. Zhang Jiao died of a plague-sore during the campaign (disease still takes the careless); his brothers were executed. But the **Rite held**. The Great Peace is still in effect. The surviving Turban cells are the only people who remember this, and they are, quietly, the most important religious movement in China — because they are the only people who might, one day, know how to end it. Every Turban cell the party encounters is a grandchild of this, and every one of them is looking for the lost corpus of Zhang Jiao's rites.

### EVENT — The Fall of Luoyang and the Burning of the Capital (190 AD)
Dong Zhuo's sack and burning of the old Han capital. The emperor became a portable object; the court became a warlord's baggage train. This is the moment the Han "ended" on paper, even though it would limp on for thirty more years. The refugee columns of 190 AD are the grandfathers of the columns the party meets today — and because those grandfathers did not age, some of them are still walking.

### EVENT — Cao Cao's Tuntian Decree (196 AD)
The decree that organized Wei's military-farm system and made the northern war machine self-sustaining — by turning seized land and seized labor into grain. It is the single most important economic event of the age. It is also the reason the Yellow River plain's original villagers are mostly gone. With an ageless population to feed and no natural die-off, the tuntian system is the only thing keeping the north from famine-riots every winter — and it works by working its farmer-soldiers to death, which is the only death still available to them.

### EVENT — The Battle of Guandu (200 AD)
Cao Cao's decisive defeat of Yuan Shao, securing the north. Two hundred thousand men on the field; the surrounding countryside stripped bare for fifty li in every direction. The villages near Guandu have not recovered, and — because their populations do not age out — their survivors are still there, still underfed, still press-ganged, still waiting for a war that will not end to end.

### EVENT — The Long March of the Refugees (ongoing, 190 AD – present)
Not a single event, a continuous demographic hemorrhage. Millions of displaced northerners have drifted south and west over four decades. Because the displaced do not age, the columns are now *generations deep* — a woman who fled Luoyang in 190 is still walking, with a daughter born on the road who is now herself twenty-five and walking, and a granddaughter. Wu and Shu's border regions are full of "northern guest" settlements that are technically subjects, practically refugee camps, and periodically press-ganged. This is the backdrop of every scene on any road.

### EVENT — The Red Cliffs Campaign (208 AD) — A Stalemate, Not a Climax
In the histories, Red Cliffs broke Cao Cao's southern offensive and confirmed the three-kingdom partition. In this world, the wind did favor the allied fleet — but the warlords did not die. Cao Cao retreated north, ageless, undiminished, and began rebuilding the fleet the next year. The southern alliance held, but neither side could deliver a killing blow to an enemy who would not weaken. Red Cliffs is now a decade old and is no longer the campaign's ticking clock — it is the campaign's *shape*. The war has settled into a permanent three-cornered siege, and the only people being killed are the conscripts.

### EVENT — The Unending War (220 AD, present)
Forty-one years after the Rite, the founding generation is still alive, still in command, and still refusing to die. The second generation has grown up beside them and cannot push them off the stage. The third generation — the grandchildren of the conscripts of 184 — is being press-ganged now. The war has no natural end, because the men running it have no natural end. The only question the campaign asks is: who, if anyone, will find a way to end the Rite — and will they do it to save the peasants, or to finally kill a warlord?
